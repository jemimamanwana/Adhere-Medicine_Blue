Understanding Diabetes

What is Diabetes?

A chronic condition — and why your medication is the single most important tool you have.

The Condition
What happens in your body
Diabetes is a chronic condition where blood glucose (blood sugar) is persistently too high. It develops when the pancreas fails to produce enough insulin, produces none at all, or when the body doesn't respond properly to the insulin it does produce. Without insulin, cells cannot absorb glucose for energy — and blood sugar accumulates to dangerous levels.
Medication Adherence
What it actually means
Medication adherence is the extent to which your medication-taking behaviour matches agreed recommendations from your healthcare provider. Right time. Right dose. Right instructions. Right duration — even when you feel completely fine.
Global Scale
422 Million
People worldwide living with diabetes as of 2022 — with numbers rising every year (WHO).
The Core Truth
Feeling fine doesn't mean cured
One of the most dangerous misconceptions — especially among young adults — is stopping medication when you feel well. That feeling is often proof the medication is working. Stopping disrupts glucose control and can trigger rapid, life-threatening complications. Consistent adherence is what keeps you well.
Classification

Three Main Types

Understanding your type defines your treatment plan and adherence strategy.

01
Type 01
Type 1 Diabetes
An autoimmune disease where the immune system attacks and destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. Produces little or no insulin. Requires lifelong insulin therapy to survive. Not caused by diet or lifestyle.
02
Type 02
Type 2 Diabetes
The most common form. The body doesn't produce enough insulin or can't use it effectively (insulin resistance). Strongly linked to lifestyle factors though genetics play a role. Managed through oral medication, insulin, or both.
03
Pregnancy-Related
Gestational Diabetes
Occurs during pregnancy when placental hormones cause insulin resistance. Usually resolves after birth but significantly raises the mother's lifetime risk of developing Type 2 diabetes. Requires careful monitoring.

What's at Stake

The Real Cost of Skipping

A missed dose feels harmless in the moment. Over months and years, uncontrolled glucose silently destroys organs.

#1
Cause of death in diabetics: cardiovascular disease
40%
Of people with diabetes will develop kidney disease
2–3×
Higher stroke risk in people with uncontrolled diabetes
80%
Of lower limb amputations are diabetes-related
Complication 01
Cardiovascular Disease
High blood glucose damages artery walls, raising risk of heart attack, stroke, and peripheral artery disease. The leading cause of death among people with diabetes globally.
Complication 02
Kidney Damage
Chronic hyperglycaemia damages the kidney's filtering vessels, progressively reducing function. Can advance to kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplant.
Complication 03
Retinopathy
Weakened retinal blood vessels cause blurred vision and can lead to permanent blindness. Diabetes is one of the world's leading causes of preventable blindness.
Complication 04
Nerve Damage
Tingling, numbness, or burning pain usually starting in hands and feet. Severe cases cause complete loss of sensation, dramatically raising the risk of undetected injury.
Complication 05
Cognitive Decline
Long-term poor glycaemic control is linked to accelerated cognitive decline and increased dementia risk — effects that appear even in young adults with persistent hyperglycaemia.
Complication 06
Foot Damage & Amputation
Combined nerve damage and poor circulation cause ulcers that heal slowly. Diabetes causes the majority of non-traumatic lower limb amputations performed worldwide every year.
Acute Emergency: Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)
When the body lacks insulin, it burns fat for energy — producing ketones that dangerously acidify the blood. DKA is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate hospitalisation. Symptoms: extreme thirst, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, and fruity-smelling breath. Poor adherence is a leading trigger.
Sources: WHO — Diabetes Fact Sheet · IDF — Diabetes Complications
The Core Case

Why Adherence Changes Everything

Consistent medication is not optional for diabetes — it is the foundation every other health outcome rests on.

01
Reason 01
Maintains normal blood glucose
Consistent medication keeps levels stable in your bloodstream, preventing hypoglycaemia (dangerous lows causing dizziness and confusion) and chronic hyperglycaemia (damaging highs that destroy blood vessels and organs over time).
02
Reason 02
Protects every blood vessel
Microvascular complications begin with weakened, leaking blood vessels. Adherence prevents this damage — protecting your eyes, kidneys, and nerves before irreversible harm occurs. Prevention is always better than treatment.
03
Reason 03
Prevents emergencies
Poor adherence triggers sudden crises like DKA or Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State — both life-threatening. Staying on track prevents these entirely.
04
Reason 04
Saves money long-term
The cost of treating complications — dialysis, eye surgery, amputations — far exceeds the cost of consistent medication. Adherence today is the most cost-effective health investment you can make.
05
Reason 05
Preserves quality of life
Good adherence keeps symptoms manageable so you can study, work, socialise, and exercise without your condition dominating every day. Control your diabetes — don't let it control you.
06
Reason 06
Slows disease progression
Maintaining healthy HbA1c through adherence consistently slows or prevents progression from manageable diabetes to severe complications. The earlier you commit, the greater the long-term dividend.
Daily Routine
Who This Is For

Young Adults with Diabetes Mellitus

Why this age group faces uniquely difficult challenges — and what makes adherence harder here than anywhere else.

Ages 15–35 · Students & Young Professionals

Why this age group struggles most

Managing diabetes requires major lifestyle adjustments that collide directly with young adulthood — exams, social life, irregular schedules, financial strain, identity formation, and the pressure to fit in. This is the group that most needs support, and most often feels alone in navigating it.

01
Cost of Medication
Many patients can't afford long-term treatment, especially insulin and newer oral drugs. Financial pressure leads to skipped doses, dose reduction, or complete discontinuation — all of which are dangerous.
02
Poor Health Literacy
Patients who don't understand their condition may stop medication when symptoms improve — wrongly believing they are cured. Informed patients adhere significantly better than uninformed ones.
03
Complex Regimens & Busy Schedules
Multiple medications at different times, exams, travel, and irregular routines all disrupt adherence patterns — particularly for students and young professionals navigating unpredictable days.
04
Side Effects & Fear of Needles
Nausea, weight gain, and needle phobia are major deterrents. Psychological resistance to insulin therapy leads to poor glycaemic control and long delays before starting necessary treatment.
05
Social & Dietary Pressure
Peers and family encourage indulgence. Cultural food norms, social events, and the desire to participate without restriction all create real friction for young adults managing dietary guidelines.
06
Denial & Short-term Thinking
Without visible consequences today, it's easy to deprioritise medication that protects future health. Some young adults don't fully accept their diagnosis or understand the long-term severity of the illness.
Practical Guidance

7 Strategies That Actually Work

Proven, practical approaches to improving adherence. No perfection required.

01
Understand your medication
Ask your nurse or doctor what each medication does, how it works, and what happens if you skip it. Patients who understand their treatment adhere significantly better than those who don't.
Write down your medication names, doses, and timing in your phone — and refer back until it's automatic.
02
Set smart, recurring reminders
Set alarms at your exact medication times. Use a dedicated medication reminder app or ask a trusted family member to check in with you daily until the habit is solid.
Medisafe, Roundhealth, and MyTherapy are all free apps built specifically for this.
03
Anchor it to an existing daily habit
Link your medication to something you already do every day — brushing teeth, morning coffee, or going to bed. The stronger the habit anchor, the less conscious effort adherence requires.
Place your medication next to your toothbrush or coffee maker as a physical cue you can't miss.
04
Address side effects — don't just stop
If you experience side effects, tell your healthcare provider. Never simply stop taking the medication. There are often alternative formulations, dose adjustments, or complementary strategies that resolve the issue.
Keep a side-effect diary (date, symptom, severity) to share at your next appointment for targeted solutions.
05
Never skip — especially on good days
Feeling fine is usually proof the medication is working. Stopping creates dangerous gaps in glucose control and can trigger rapid deterioration. Consistency is the entire point of chronic disease management.
Missed a dose? Take it as soon as you remember — unless it's near your next dose time. Never double up.
06
Refill your prescription early
Running out creates dangerous treatment gaps. Always maintain at least a week's supply as a buffer. Many pharmacies offer automatic refill programmes — ask your pharmacist about enrolling.
Set a phone reminder when you're down to your last 7 days of supply — before you even think about running out.
07
Build a support network
Talk openly with family and friends about your treatment needs. Emotional support significantly improves long-term adherence. Consider joining a peer diabetes group — the IDF runs programmes specifically for young adults.
The International Diabetes Federation has young adult peer programmes: idf.org
